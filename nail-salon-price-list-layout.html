How do I lay out a nail salon price list so clients can read it fast?

Updated 2026-11-08

A price list is often the first thing a new client reads. If it is hard to scan, clients ask questions you have already answered, or they leave without booking. A clear layout saves time at the front desk and on the phone. Here is a simple checklist you can use on a printed menu, a website page, or a social post.

Why layout matters

Most people do not read a price list line by line. They scan for one service, find the price, and decide. If the list is crowded, mixed up, or hard to follow, they give up or call to ask. Good layout cuts that friction. It also makes your salon look organised, which matters when a new client is choosing where to go.

Group services the way clients search

Start with the main categories that clients already use to think about nail services. Common groups are manicures, pedicures, gel and extensions, nail art, and waxing. Keep each group in its own block, with a clear heading.

  • Put the most booked service at the top of each group.
  • Keep names the same as what you say on the phone, so clients do not have to translate.
  • Use plain words. "Gel manicure" is easier to find than an internal code name.
  • Do not put one service in two groups. Clients will wonder which one is correct.

Make prices easy to find

The price should sit on the same line as the service name, so the eye does not jump across the page. Use a consistent format for every row, such as the service name, a dotted line or space, then the price. Consistency helps people read faster, because they learn the pattern after the first few lines.

  • Put the price on the same line as the service name.
  • Use one format for every row, for example "Gel manicure ... $XX".
  • Show add-ons, such as nail art, length changes, or removal, as their own short list rather than inside a paragraph.
  • If a price depends on length or a choice, say so in the group heading, not in fine print.

Keep it short

Long lists are harder to scan. Remove services you no longer offer, and merge items that are almost the same. A short list that matches what you actually do is better than a long one that includes old options. Put policies, such as deposit or cancellation rules, on a separate line or a separate card. Do not mix them into the price rows, because a client who sees a policy in the middle of a price list may miss both.

Match your print and digital versions

If you have a printed menu and a digital one, they should match. Update both on the same day. A client who sees one price online and another on the wall will lose trust fast. Keep a single master copy, and copy changes from it to every version, rather than editing each one by hand.

Check it before you share it

Before you publish or print, run a short test.

  • Read the list out loud once. If a line makes you pause, rewrite it.
  • Ask one client or a friend to find one price in under ten seconds. If they cannot, fix the layout.
  • Look at it on a phone screen as well as on paper. Text that is fine on a laptop can be too small on a phone.
  • Confirm every price against your own current menu. Prices change, and this article does not list any prices.

Keep it up to date

Set a simple routine. Review the list every time you change a price, add a service, or drop one. A quick check once a season also catches small errors that build up over time.
