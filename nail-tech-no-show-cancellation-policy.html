What should a nail tech no-show and cancellation policy say?

Updated 2026-10-22

Most no-show policies fail for one reason: the client never read them. The second reason is the tech didn't want to enforce them.

A good policy fits in one text message, covers the three things that happen (cancel early, cancel late, vanish), and says what happens to the money in each case.

This is general information, not legal advice. Every policy wording below is an example — check it against your state's rules and your booking platform's terms before you use it.

The four parts every policy needs

  1. The deposit. How much, taken when.
  2. The notice window. How much warning you need before a cancellation is free.
  3. Late cancellation. What happens to the deposit.
  4. No-show. What happens, and whether they can rebook.

Everything else is decoration.

A worked example you can adapt

> Booking > A $20 deposit is required to confirm your appointment and comes off your final price. > > Rescheduling or cancelling > More than 24 hours' notice: your deposit moves to your new appointment, or comes back to you. > Less than 24 hours' notice: the deposit is kept, and can be used against a new booking within 30 days. > > No-shows > No message and no arrival: the deposit is kept and no credit carries over. A second no-show means booking requires full prepayment. > > Late arrival > More than 15 minutes late may mean the service is shortened or moved, so the next client isn't delayed. > > Fees > Any no-show fee or late fee is in addition to the deposit and is explained before it is charged.

Why the notice window is the whole game

Cancellations with 48+ hours' notice and no-shows are not the same event. One gives you time to refill the slot; the other costs you the slot entirely. A common framework:

  • 48 hours or more: full deposit refund or free transfer
  • Under 48 hours: deposit held, sometimes usable as credit toward a rebooking within 30 days
  • No-show, no notice: deposit forfeited, no credit

Twenty-four hours instead of 48 is fine, as long as you apply it to everyone.

Keep the charging side proportionate

If a policy charges more than the loss it's covering, it gets shaky fast. Where a client cancels at short notice and you refill the slot, you have not lost the income — and a charge that ignores that is the kind of term that gets challenged. That's the same logic behind the fairness and transparency expectations in consumer protection rules in the UK (Consumer Rights Act 2015) and similar regimes elsewhere. Keep the charge tied to the time you actually lost.

Put it where clients will see it

  • Booking page: the policy, in the same place as the deposit request
  • Instagram bio or highlights: a one-line version
  • First message after they book: repeat the deposit and the notice window
  • Reminder text 24–48 hours before: "Reminder for tomorrow at 2pm. Your deposit is on, see you then."

Say it out loud, once

When someone cancels late, one short message, no lecture:

> Hey [name], no problem — but your deposit covers that slot, so it stays with the appointment. Want me to look at next week instead?

That's the entire enforcement conversation. It only works if you've already written it down.
