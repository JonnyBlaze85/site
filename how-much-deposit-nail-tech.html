How much deposit should a nail tech charge?

Updated 2026-10-22

A deposit isn't a punishment. It's rent on a chair nobody else can use while it's held for you. So the right deposit is the smallest amount that makes a client show up.

Plenty of techs never take one because they don't know what number is "normal". Here's the honest answer, and the trade-offs.

This is general information, not legal or tax advice, and the wording below is an example. Rules on deposits, fees and cancellation charges differ by state and country — check yours before you rely on any of it.

The three models techs actually use

1. Flat fee. One amount for every booking. Easy to explain, easy to remember, easy to enforce. Example: $20 holds any appointment.

2. Percentage. A slice of the service price. Keeps things fair when your menu runs from a $35 fill to a $120 full set with art. Deposit guides for beauty businesses commonly land in the 20–50% range, with 25–30% a common middle.

3. Full prepay. The whole service paid at booking. It kills no-shows almost entirely, but it's a hard ask for first-time clients.

A simple way to choose

  • Under about two hours of your time: a flat fee is usually enough. It's about commitment, not compensation.
  • Long appointments (full sets, extensions, nail art by the hour): a percentage, or the flat fee plus a note that the slot is longer.
  • Brand new client, first booking: the deposit matters most here. Regulars are not the risk.
  • Repeat regular who has never flaked: you can loosen it. Many techs waive the deposit after a client has kept three bookings in a row.

What the deposit should equal

Don't tie your deposit to the price of the service. Tie it to what the empty slot costs you:

  • Your hourly rate × the hours booked
  • Plus any product you'd already prepped or ordered
  • Minus what you might realistically re-fill at short notice

If a two-hour set is $120 and you can't refill a same-day cancellation, a $20 deposit makes the client care. It does not make you whole. That's fine — the point is to prevent the loss, not to profit from it.

Take it at booking, not the day before

The deposit should be collected when the appointment is made, not later. On booking platforms, a deposit requirement collects the payment before the booking is confirmed, so you never chase anyone. If you book by message, send the payment link immediately and say the slot is only held once the deposit clears — 24 hours is a normal window.

Word it the same way every time

Pick one sentence and use it everywhere: booking page, Instagram bio, reminder text. An example you can adapt:

> A $20 deposit is required to book and comes off your final price. Cancel or move your appointment with 24 hours' notice and it moves with you. Less than 24 hours, or no notice at all, and the deposit is kept.

Watch what your platform allows

  • Square Appointments collects deposits when the client books online. It does not support taking a deposit on an appointment you add to the calendar yourself.
  • Booksy has keyed-in payment, which lets you enter a client's card details manually, so you can take a deposit on appointments you add yourself.

The one rule that matters

A deposit you enforce beats a bigger deposit you're too polite to keep. Start low, be consistent, and raise it once you've said no to someone once or twice.
